M13 Embeddings and model selection

An embedding model turns each chunk into a fixed-length list of numbers, and retrieval ranks chunks by how close those lists are to the question’s. The model therefore decides what “similar” means for your corpus, and changing it later means re-embedding every chunk. The M13 notebooks build on four ideas: how to compare two vectors, what the number of dimensions costs, how to stop paying twice for the same vector, and how to tell whether a model fits your documents.

Open the lab

The running example is the Larkspur Cloud handbook from M10 to M12, cut into the chunks the M10 lab uses, and the same three golden questions: How long does a refund take?, Can I export my data after I cancel my plan? and What happens if my server goes down? The lab adds one chunk that answers none of them: plans-overview, the long pricing page kept as a single chunk.

TL;DR

  • Check the norm before you choose a metric. OpenAI returns unit-length vectors, and for those cosine, dot product and Euclidean distance rank identically. sentence-transformers does not normalize by default; there, dot product favours longer vectors. In the lab, stretching one off-topic chunk to 1.15× its length is enough for dot product to rank it above the refund answer.
  • Dimensions are a cost paid on every query. text-embedding-3-small returns 1536 numbers, -large 3072. At four bytes each, a million chunks take 6.1 GB at 1536 and 12.3 GB at 3072 before any index overhead. The dimensions parameter shortens the vector; measure what that costs on your own questions.
  • Cache embeddings, and namespace the cache by model and dimensions. CacheBackedEmbeddings keys each vector by namespace + sha256(text). Re-embedding the 12 handbook chunks after one edit sends 1 text to the model instead of 12. Without a namespace, a model swap silently returns the old model’s vectors.
  • Domain fit is measured on your own golden set, not read off a leaderboard. The same model must embed documents and queries; a mismatch raises no error and returns noise.

Mental model

A chunk becomes a vector once, at index time, and the cache makes sure it stays once. A question becomes a vector on every request, and it has to come from the same model with the same dimensions as the stored vectors; otherwise the scores compare two unrelated coordinate systems. Recording the model name and dimensions beside the vectors is how a query service can check that before it searches.

Cosine similarity

M10 used embeddings to find the refund and outage evidence that TF-IDF missed, and M12 decided what text each vector stands for. This module opens the embedding step itself: what the numbers mean, how they are compared, and which model produces them.

In the lab for this lesson, the Larkspur pricing page is drawn slightly longer than the other chunks, as an unnormalised model would leave it. Asked How long does a refund take?, a dot-product search ranks the pricing page above refund-timing, the chunk that holds the answer. Nothing about the pricing page changed except its length.

Retrieval needs one number per chunk that says how close it is to the question. Three measures are in common use, and every vector store offers at least one of them:

Measure How it is computed What it responds to Closer chunk
Cosine similarity The dot product divided by both vectors’ lengths The angle only; range -1 to 1 Higher
Dot product The sum of the products of matching numbers The angle and both lengths Higher
Euclidean distance The straight-line gap between the two vector tips The angle and both lengths Lower

Table 1: The three similarity measures.

Cosine measures direction only, and a vector’s length cannot change it. The dot product is cosine multiplied by both lengths, so a longer vector scores higher at the same angle. Euclidean distance measures the gap between the tips, so a longer vector moves away:

Figure 1: One question and one chunk. Stretching the chunk to 1.5× leaves its cosine at 0.866, raises its dot product from 0.866 to 1.299, and pushes its distance from 0.518 to 0.807.

When both vectors have length 1, the three agree. The dot product equals the cosine, and Euclidean distance is √(2 - 2·cos), which falls as cosine rises. All three then return the same chunks in the same order. Notebook 13.1 checks this on two real text-embedding-3-small vectors. That is why the first thing to print about a new model is the norm (length) of one of its vectors:

Model output Norm Which metric
OpenAI text-embedding-3-* 1.0, give or take float rounding Any of the three; pick the one your store computes fastest, usually dot product
sentence-transformers through the Hugging Face wrapper, default settings Not 1 Cosine, or turn on normalisation and then any of the three

Table 2: The norm check, and the metric it allows.

The interactive lab draws ten Larkspur handbook chunks as 2D vectors so the angles are visible. The vectors are hand-placed and every score is computed; the lab’s cosine, dot and Euclidean values match scikit-learn’s to within 2e-15 at all 156 settings. Every chunk has length 1 except plans-overview, the long pricing page, whose length the slider sets, the way notebook 13.1 scales one vector by 3.0 to imitate a model that does not normalise:

plans-overview length How long does a refund take? Can I export my data after I cancel? What happens if my server goes down?
1.0× All three rank refund-timing first All three rank retention first All three rank sla first
1.2× Dot product ranks plans-overview first Dot product moves it from #4 to #3 No change
1.5× Dot: plans-overview first; Euclidean: #4 Dot: plans-overview first; Euclidean: #5 Euclidean moves it to last
3.0× Dot: first; Euclidean: last of 10 Dot: first; Euclidean: last of 10 Dot and Euclidean both rank it last

Table 3: Where plans-overview ranks as its length grows, from the lab.

We call this length bias: a ranking that follows vector length instead of meaning. The table shows three things about it:

  • A small length difference reorders close scores. For the refund question, dot product prefers the pricing page from 1.15× onward. Its cosine with the question is 0.866 against refund-timing’s 0.998, and that gap is all the stretch has to overcome.
  • Cosine never moves. In every row, cosine ranks the evidence first and plans-overview third or worse. Normalising every vector first gives the same result for all three metrics.
  • Length cannot rescue a wrong direction. For the outage question plans-overview points away (cosine -0.122), so a longer vector only makes its dot product more negative.

Two cautions apply whatever the metric. Cosine scores are not probabilities, and their range differs between models. A threshold such as “answer only above 0.7” (notebook 13.2’s optional tutorial gives bands like this) has to be re-measured after a model change. Embeddings also encode topic more than truth: notebook 13.1 warns that a sentence and its negation embed close together, so “the plan is refundable” and “the plan is not refundable” can retrieve each other. We call that negation blindness. Negation, numeric comparison and exact identifiers such as invoice numbers are weak spots, which hybrid search in M16 addresses.

The same model on both sides

The model that embeds the chunks must also embed the questions, with the same dimensions setting. Breaking this rule raises no error. Notebook 13.1 indexes with text-embedding-3-small, queries with text-embedding-3-large truncated to 1536 dimensions, and gets vectors of the same shape on both sides. The search runs and returns scores from two unrelated coordinate systems:

Figure 2: A silent model mismatch. The shapes match, and nothing stops the search.

We call this a silent model mismatch. The tell is the score itself: if the best match for a question that plainly has an answer in the corpus scores near zero, suspect a mismatch before you touch chunking or prompts. It happens three ways in practice:

  1. Config drift. The indexing job and the query service read the model name from different places, and only one gets updated.
  2. An unnamespaced cache. The cache returns the old model’s vectors for the new model’s requests (Embedding cache, lesson 13.4).
  3. A provider-side update. A pinned model name is not always pinned weights.

The defence is cheap. Write the model name and dimensions into the index metadata when you build it, and have the query service compare them with its own configuration before it serves a request.

In practice: choosing the metric for the Larkspur index

For the handbook index, the metric follows from two checks:

  1. Print the norm of one chunk vector. For text-embedding-3-small it is 1.0. Cosine, dot product and Euclidean then rank the same, and you can use the one the store computes fastest.
  2. If a model returns other norms, normalise at embedding time, before length bias can reorder the refund question.
  3. Store the model name and dimensions in the index metadata, and check them at query time.
  4. Add a negation case to the golden set, such as a monthly-plan refund question, to watch for negation blindness.

Next: lesson 13.2 looks at the hosted models themselves, and what their dimensions cost to store and search.

Key Takeaways

  • Print the norm of one vector from any new model. With unit-length vectors, cosine, dot product and Euclidean distance rank chunks identically; otherwise use cosine or normalise first.
  • Cosine scores are not probabilities, and embeddings capture topic more than truth. Negation, numeric comparison and exact identifiers are weak spots.
  • Embed chunks and questions with the same model and dimensions. A mismatch raises no error; record both in the index metadata and check them before serving.

2. In the lab, the plans-overview vector is stretched to 1.5 times its length. Which chunk does dot product rank first for the refund-timing question, and what does cosine do?

Dot product ranks plans-overview first. It overtakes from 1.15× onward, because its cosine of 0.866 is not far below refund-timing’s 0.998. Cosine never moves and keeps refund-timing first. Normalising every vector makes all three metrics agree with cosine.

3. After a config change, the top match for an answerable question scores near zero, and no error is raised. What do you suspect first, and what prevents it?

A model mismatch: chunks and questions embedded by different models, such as text-embedding-3-small and text-embedding-3-large truncated to 1536, give vectors of the same shape from unrelated coordinate systems. Vector dimensionality

Lesson 13.2: Hosted embeddings: OpenAI models and dimensions. Notebooks: 02a_openai_embeddings.ipynb, 02b_openai_embeddings_tutorial.ipynb (optional)

What you’ll learn

  • Explain what an embedding’s dimensionality is, and why every vector in one index must share it.
  • Compare the one-off cost of embedding a corpus with the running cost of storing and searching it.
  • Measure a dimension cut by its margin, not only by recall@1.
  • Choose a model and dimension count, knowing that changing either means re-embedding.

Lesson 13.1 showed how vectors are compared. This lesson looks at the hosted models that produce them, and at the one number that sets most of an index’s running cost.

A teammate moves the Larkspur index from text-embedding-3-small to text-embedding-3-large because “bigger is better”. The embedding bill barely moves. The vector store’s memory doubles, every search does twice the arithmetic, and nobody measured whether the handbook’s questions retrieve any better.

An embedding’s dimensionality is how many numbers the model returns per text. It is fixed by the model, or by the dimensions setting described below, and every stored vector and every query vector in one index must have the same count. The notebooks use three hosted OpenAI models:

Model Default dimensions Max input tokens Listed price per 1M tokens 1,000 chunks of 500 characters
text-embedding-3-small 1536 8191 $0.02 $0.0025
text-embedding-3-large 3072 8191 $0.13 $0.016
text-embedding-ada-002 (previous generation) 1536 8191 $0.10 $0.0125

Table 1: The three hosted models in the notebooks, with the cost of embedding 1,000 short chunks.

Dimensions, token limits and prices are as the lesson 13.2 notebooks list them. Check the provider’s current price list before you budget. The last column is arithmetic: 500 characters is about 125 tokens at four characters per token, the rule of thumb the M10 lab uses, so 1,000 such chunks are about 125,000 tokens.

Embedding the corpus is a one-off bill, and small. The dimensions are paid again on every query, for as long as the index exists. The store keeps every number, holds them in memory to search fast, and multiplies through them for each similarity. Stored as 32-bit floats, before any index overhead or compression:

Dimensions Bytes per vector 1 million chunks
384 (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, lesson 13.3) 1,536 1.5 GB
1024 (BAAI/bge-large-en, lesson 13.3) 4,096 4.1 GB
1536 (3-small) 6,144 6.1 GB
3072 (3-large) 12,288 12.3 GB

Table 2: Memory for one million vectors at four dimension counts (4 bytes per number).

Notebook 13.1 puts it as a rule: a 3072-dimension index is twice the size of a 1536-dimension one and costs roughly twice as much per search. Some vector stores can compress vectors into fewer bytes per number, which lowers these figures at some cost in accuracy.

Shortening a vector with dimensions

The text-embedding-3 models are trained with Matryoshka representation learning, which puts the most useful information in the first numbers. The API can therefore cut a vector short and re-normalise it, and the shorter vector still works for search. Two settings the notebooks use:

Model and dimensions setting What you get
3-small at 512 512 numbers instead of 1536, a third of the index
3-large at 1536 The large model at the small model’s index size

Table 3: Two uses of the dimensions setting.

The cut happens inside the API, before the vector reaches the store:

Figure 1: Truncating a 3-large vector to 1536 dimensions.

Shorter vectors are worse. The open question is how much worse on your data for what you save. Notebook 13.1 measures it on six labelled queries at 1536, 512, 256, 128, 64 and 32 dimensions and reports two numbers per setting:

  • Recall@1, which saturates on an easy set: six well-separated documents score perfectly at almost any size, and then the metric says nothing more. We call that saturated recall.
  • Margin, the correct document’s similarity minus the best wrong document’s. It keeps shrinking as dimensions fall, and a shrinking margin is where recall will break on a large corpus with near-duplicates, such as Larkspur’s refund-window and refund-timing chunks.

The notebook’s most useful row is 3-large truncated to 1536: a stronger model at the same index cost as 3-small. Run the same comparison on the M10 golden set before you pick a size. Treat the choice as permanent, because changing dimensions means re-embedding every chunk and querying with the new setting.

In practice: sizing the Larkspur index

For the handbook, the model and dimension choice takes four steps:

  1. Shortlist 3-small at 1536 and 3-large truncated to 1536, which cost the same to store and search.
  2. Score both on the M10 golden set by recall and margin, paying attention to the near-duplicate refund chunks.
  3. Try a shorter setting, such as 512, only if memory is the constraint, and keep it only if the margin holds.
  4. Record the model and dimensions in the index metadata (lesson 13.1), since every query must use the same pair.

Key Takeaways

  • Embedding the corpus is a small one-off bill; dimensions are paid on every query in memory and compute. One million 1536-dimension vectors take about 6.1 GB as 32-bit floats.
  • The text-embedding-3 models can be shortened with the dimensions setting. 3-large at 1536 gives a stronger model at the same index cost as 3-small.
  • Judge a dimension cut by the margin, not only recall@1, which saturates on easy sets. Treat the choice as permanent, because changing it means re-embedding every chunk.

Domain fit

  • Compare open models run locally with hosted ones by cost, data, latency and operations.

Lesson 13.2 sized the hosted OpenAI models. Open models run on your own machines, which widens the choice, and makes the question of which one fits your documents harder to skip.

A Larkspur customer asks How much bandwidth does the Business plan include? The handbook never says “bandwidth”. It says “outbound traffic”. If the embedding model does not place those two phrases close together, the plans page loses to whatever chunk happens to mention networks, and the answer comes from the wrong page. Nothing raises an error.

A model has domain fit when texts that mean the same thing in your documents land close together and texts that differ land apart. General-purpose models are trained on broad web text. They can place two Larkspur chunks that differ by one condition (a full refund within 30 days, a prorated one after) almost on top of each other, miss a customer’s synonym for a handbook term, or fail on a language they were not trained on. We call any of these a domain miss: it shows up only as the wrong chunk ranked first.

Two settings decide whether a local model behaves like the hosted ones in lessons 13.1 and 13.2:

  • Normalisation is off by default. Turn it on in the wrapper’s encode settings, or score with cosine. Otherwise dot product rewards longer vectors, as the lesson 13.1 lab shows. We call a local model left this way an unnormalised local model.
  • Some models are asymmetric. They are trained so that short questions and long passages meet, and they treat the two sides differently: embed_query and embed_documents can return different vectors for the same text. Keep the two calls separate in your code even when today’s model is symmetric.

Hosted or self-hosted is part of the fit, because some corpora may not leave your network. The trade, from notebook 13.1:

Latency A network round trip Local compute; a GPU helps a lot

Deprecation The provider can retire the model, forcing a re-index You decide when it changes

Leaderboards such as MTEB (the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark) average many public datasets. They are good for a shortlist. The decision comes from scoring the shortlist on your own documents and questions, and a short decision chain narrows where to start:

Every shortlist then goes through the same scoring on the golden set: recall@k, margin, cost and latency. A useful golden set for Larkspur holds the M10 questions plus hard negatives: chunks on the same topic that do not answer the question, such as cancel for Can I export my data after I cancel my plan? In practice: an open-model shortlist for Larkspur

For the handbook, choosing an open model takes four steps:

  1. Shortlist all-MiniLM-L6-v2, all-mpnet-base-v2 and bge-large-en from Table 1, plus the hosted model from lesson 13.2 as the baseline.
  2. Turn on normalisation for every open model, so the metric from lesson 13.1 behaves.

Key Takeaways

  • Poor domain fit raises no error; it shows up as the wrong chunk ranked first. Use leaderboards for a shortlist and your own golden set, with hard negatives, for the decision.
  • The Hugging Face wrapper does not normalise by default. Turn normalisation on or score with cosine, and keep embed_query and embed_documents separate for asymmetric models.
  • Hosted models cost per token and send data off your network; self-hosted ones cost hardware and operations. Decide which metric matters before you compare results.

Embedding cache

Lesson 13.4: Production concerns: batching, caching and similarity behaviour. Notebooks: 04a_embeddings_deep_dive.ipynb

What you’ll learn

  • Explain how an embedding cache decides whether a text has been embedded before.
  • Trace five indexing runs through the cache: what reaches the model and what the store holds.
  • Diagnose a model swap that re-embeds nothing and breaks retrieval.
  • Choose batched calls over per-chunk calls for indexing.

Lessons 13.1 to 13.3 chose a metric, a model and its dimensions. This lesson is about running that choice every day: re-indexing as the handbook changes, without paying for the same vectors twice or mixing two models’ vectors in one index.

The Larkspur team swaps embedding models and re-indexes the handbook. The job finishes in seconds, having sent none of the 12 chunks to the new model, and retrieval quality collapses. The cache answered every request with the old model’s vectors, because both models wrote to the same cache keys.

Every re-run of an indexing notebook, and every re-ingest after a change elsewhere in the pipeline, embeds the whole corpus again and pays for it again, for vectors identical to the ones you already had. An embedding cache wraps an embedding model with a key-value store: text it has seen comes back from the store, and only new text reaches the model. LangChain’s cache-backed embeddings work this way, and the notebook sets them up with five choices:

Setting In the notebook Why it matters
Underlying model text-embedding-3-small The model that is called, and billed, on a miss
Store A local file store in a folder One file per vector; any other byte store, such as Redis, can serve a cache shared between workers
Namespace The model name and its dimensions Keeps two models’ vectors under different keys
Key encoder SHA-256 The default, SHA-1, raises a warning
Query caching Off, the default Few questions repeat word for word

Table 1: The cache settings, as notebook 13.4 sets them.

Each lookup builds a key from the namespace and a hash of the text:

Figure 1: One cache lookup. Only a miss reaches the billed model.

A cache entry therefore matches only the exact text under the exact namespace. To see what that means for the Larkspur handbook, the 12 chunks of the M10 lab were run through the real cache-backed embeddings with a counting stand-in for the API (LangChain’s deterministic fake embedding, so the counts are exact and free):

Run Texts sent to the model Keys in the store
1. Index the 12 chunks 12 of 12 12
2. Index the same 12 again 0 of 12, identical vectors 12
3. Edit one chunk (“100 GB” to “200 GB”), index again 1 of 12 13
4. A second model under its own namespace 12 of 12 25
5. A second model, both with no namespace 0 of 12: every vector is the first model’s 12, shared by both

Table 2: Five indexing runs through the cache on the 12 handbook chunks.

Each run shows one property of the cache:

  • Re-indexing after an edit costs the edit. When M12’s chunking changes only some chunks, only those are embedded again. Chunks whose text is unchanged hit the cache.
  • Old entries stay. Run 3 leaves the pre-edit vector in the store. A file store grows until you clear it. Give it a home you can delete or expire. We call these stale entries.
  • The namespace is part of correctness. The cache defaults to an empty namespace. Run 5 swaps the model and gets the old model’s vectors back with no error: an index mixing two vector spaces, the mismatch from lesson 13.1. We call this a shared namespace, and it is the opening failure.

Notebook 13.4 writes its cache to a temporary folder, and the cache is discarded when the cell ends.

Batching

The document method sends many texts in one request, and the query method sends one. Looping the query method over a corpus pays one network round trip per chunk, and on a real corpus that is the difference between minutes and hours. We call this per-chunk round trips. Use embed_documents for the index and embed_query for questions.

Notebook 13.1 runs both paths on the same six texts and compares the vectors:

Comparison Result
Cosine between the two vectors for the same text About 1.0: semantically identical
Exact equality Not bit-identical: a batched request and a single request take different paths through the server

Table 3: Batched and single embeddings of the same text.

The difference is numerical noise. Never use exact equality to decide whether two vectors came from the same text. The cache’s hash of the text does that job. For an asymmetric model the two methods differ on purpose, which is one more reason to keep the two calls separate (lesson 13.3).

In practice: the Larkspur nightly re-index

For the handbook’s nightly re-index, the cache and batching settings come down to five decisions:

  1. Namespace the cache by model and dimensions, such as text-embedding-3-small at 1536. A model swap then starts a fresh set of keys.
  2. Embed in batches with the document method, never a loop of single calls.
  3. Expect only edited chunks to reach the model. After a one-chunk edit, 1 of 12 texts is billed.
  4. Expire the store on a schedule, or delete a namespace when its model is retired, to clear stale entries.
  5. Use a shared byte store, such as Redis, once more than one worker indexes.

Key Takeaways

  • An embedding cache keys each vector on namespace plus a hash of the text, so re-indexing after an edit pays only for the changed chunks. Old entries stay until you clear them.
  • Put the model name and dimensions in the cache namespace. The default empty namespace silently returns the old model’s vectors after a model swap.
  • Embed the corpus with batched embed_documents, not a loop of embed_query. The two paths differ by numerical noise, so never compare vectors for exact equality.

Check yourself

1. Why should an indexing job call embed documents on a batch instead of looping embed query, and why not compare the two outputs for exact equality?

embed_documents sends many texts in one request, while looping embed_query pays one network round trip per chunk, the difference between minutes and hours on a real corpus. The two paths return vectors that are semantically identical (cosine about 1.0) but not bit-identical, because they take different paths through the server. Never use exact equality to decide whether two vectors came from the same text; that is the cache’s job.

2. You edit one of the 12 Larkspur chunks from 100 GB to 200 GB and re-index through the cache. How many texts reach the model, and how many keys are stored?

One of 12 texts reaches the model, and the store holds 13 keys. The edited text hashes to a new key, and the pre-edit vector stays in the store, which grows until you clear it.

3. After you swap embedding models, re-indexing sends zero texts to the model and retrieval quality collapses. What is wrong?

Both models share the cache’s default empty namespace, so the cache returns the first model’s vectors for the second model’s requests. The index now mixes two vector spaces, with no error. Put the model name and its dimensions setting in the namespace.

The project: an embedding model bake-off on Databricks

Lesson 13.5: Benchmarking embedding models with MLflow (Databricks). Notebooks: 05a_compare_embedding_models_databricks.ipynb (optional)

What you’ll build

  • Embeddings of one small corpus from three open models, stored side by side in one table.
  • One vector search index per model, so each model is searched on its own vectors.
  • A retriever per model that embeds the question with that same model and returns the top 5.
  • An MLflow evaluation that grades every model’s retrieval with two automatic judges.

Lesson 13.3 ended with a rule: shortlist models, then score them on your own questions. This project runs that scoring on a managed platform. Three open models from lesson 13.3’s family embed the same eight documents, Databricks Vector Search serves each model’s vectors, and MLflow’s judges grade what each model retrieves for 15 queries.

Out of the notebook, the change is scale and bookkeeping. Each model gets its own index and its own MLflow run. The comparison sits in one experiment that anyone on the team can reopen, and a new candidate model is one more run.

Limits

  • Eight documents and 15 queries cannot settle a model choice. They show the method. The corpus is generic machine-learning sentences, not the Larkspur handbook, and it has no hard negatives (lesson 13.3).
  • Groundedness grades retrieved text here. The app returns the retrieved text itself as its response, so groundedness measures how well that excerpt matches its own sources, not an LLM’s answer.
  • No labelled evidence. Both scores come from LLM judges, and there is no recall@k against known evidence chunks. Run the M10 golden set beside the judges.
  • What it needs to run: a Databricks workspace with Vector Search and MLflow’s GenAI evaluation. The catalog and schema names are hard-coded to one user’s workspace and must be changed, and the three indexes are created by hand in the UI before the evaluation cell runs.

Key Takeaways

  • Give each candidate model its own index and its own evaluation run, and embed every question with the same model as its index.
  • Use more than one judge: on this run the smallest model tied for best relevance while another model scored best on groundedness.
  • Treat a small, unlabelled bake-off as a method check. The decision needs your own golden set, with hard negatives and labelled evidence.

Check yourself

1. Why does each retriever in the bake-off embed the question with its own index's model?

Each index holds vectors from one model, and vectors from different models live in unrelated spaces. Embedding the question with any other model would give scores without meaning and no error, the silent model mismatch from lesson 13.1.

2. MiniLM and GTE tie for best retrieval relevance at 0.36, while BGE scores best on groundedness at 0.8667. Which model do you choose?

It depends on which metric the product needs, which you should decide before reading the results. Eight documents and 15 queries also cannot settle the choice, so rerun the comparison on your own golden set before committing.

3. A colleague cites the groundedness scores as proof that BGE gives more trustworthy answers. What is wrong with that reading?

The app returns the retrieved text itself as its response, so groundedness here grades that excerpt against its own sources, not an LLM’s answer. The scores say nothing about answer quality. Measure that with a real generation step and the golden set.

Interview Spotlight: 5 Questions You Might Get Asked

Production questions of the kind asked in applied AI engineering interviews.

1. Larkspur’s support assistant was fine on Friday. On Monday every answer is vague, and the logged top-1 similarity for golden questions has fallen from its usual range to near zero. Nobody touched the prompt. What do you check first?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Suspects a model mismatch before a quality problem: scores collapsing toward zero on questions that clearly have answers is the signature
  • Compares the model name and dimensions the query service uses with the ones recorded in the index metadata; matching dimensions prove nothing, since 3-large truncated to 1536 has the same shape as 3-small
  • Checks the usual causes: config read from two places, an unnamespaced embedding cache, a provider-side model update
  • Adds a startup check that refuses to serve when the query model differs from the index model, and keeps the golden-set run in the deploy pipeline

2. The handbook must stay in the EU, and the team proposes replacing text-embedding-3-small with self-hosted all-MiniLM-L6-v2. What do you need to know before you agree?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Data residency may decide it, but the quality cost still has to be measured: 384 dimensions against 1536, on the M10 golden set plus hard negatives such as the two refund chunks
  • Checks normalization: sentence-transformers does not normalize by default, so either pass normalize_embeddings=True or make sure the store uses cosine
  • Counts the operational cost: serving, scaling, GPU or CPU capacity, model versioning, in exchange for no per-token bill and no provider deprecations
  • Plans the migration as a full re-index into a new collection, with a new cache namespace, switched over only after the golden set passes

3. Storage for the vector index is the biggest line on the bill. Someone suggests dimensions=256. How do you decide?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Computes the saving first: 256 against 1536 dimensions is one sixth of the raw vector storage and similarity work
  • Measures recall@k and the margin between the correct chunk and the best wrong one at each candidate size; recall saturates on easy sets while the margin keeps shrinking
  • Tries 3-large truncated to the same size as an alternative: a stronger model at the same index cost
  • Remembers that truncation is a re-index, so it is decided once on real traffic samples, not tuned weekly

4. A new embedding model goes live. How would you notice in production, within a day, that it retrieves worse than the old one?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Keeps the old index serving in shadow and logs both top-k lists for a sample of real queries
  • Tracks the top-1 score distribution and the share of queries under a score floor, recalibrated for the new model because score ranges differ between models
  • Runs the golden set and an LLM-judged sample of live traffic on both, comparing recall and answer faithfulness
  • Watches slices separately: other languages, product names and numeric questions can regress while the average improves

5. The vector store is configured for dot product, and the embedding model is a sentence-transformers model loaded with default settings. Retrieval keeps returning the longest pages in the handbook. Explain the bug and the fix.

What a strong answer covers:

  • Without normalization, vector length varies, and dot product scores length as well as direction; cosine scores direction only
  • Confirms it by printing the norms of the stored vectors, which a normalized model keeps at 1.0
  • Fixes it by normalizing at embedding time (encode_kwargs={"normalize_embeddings": True}) or by switching the store to cosine, then re-indexes
  • Adds a test that asserts unit norms on a sample of stored vectors, so a config change cannot bring the bug back

Key Takeaways

  • Print a vector’s norm before you choose a metric. With unit vectors, use whichever metric your store computes fastest; without them, use cosine or normalize first.
  • Dimensions cost storage and search time on every query. Shrink them only after measuring the margin on your own golden set.
  • Wrap the embedder in CacheBackedEmbeddings with a namespace that names the model and its dimensions, so re-runs and small edits cost almost nothing.
  • Choose a model on your own documents and questions, use the same model on both sides, and record it in the index metadata so a mismatch fails loudly.
