M18 Query transformation and routing

Users ask short, vague or compound questions, and the retriever searches with exactly what they typed. M18 changes the query before retrieval: rewrite it, ask it several ways, ask a broader question, search with an invented answer, split it, or send it to the right place first. The M18 notebooks build on four ideas: why a question and its answer sit far apart, how to fuse rankings from several phrasings, how a hypothetical answer becomes a search key, and the two ways to route a question.

Open the lab

The running example is the Larkspur Cloud handbook from M11: twelve chunks on billing, refunds, outages, servers and data exports, and the same three golden questions. M11 left one of them unsolved. What happens if my server goes down? shares no word with the two chunks that answer it, so no value of k could retrieve them. M18 starts there.

TL;DR

  • Questions and documents differ in vocabulary (“server goes down” against “outage” and “availability”) and in shape (a nine-word question against a 400-token passage). Every technique here closes one of those gaps, or splits a question that has more than one need.
  • Multi-query and RAG-Fusion search with several LLM-written phrasings. In the lab, five variants per question lift mean recall@3 over the three golden questions from 0.50 to 0.83 with a flat union and to 1.00 with RRF.
  • RRF’s constant k is a real setting. At two chunks sent, k = 60 scores 0.67 and k = 1 scores 0.83, because a large k lets chunks that several variants rank low outvote a chunk two variants rank high.
  • HyDE embeds an LLM-written answer instead of the question. It costs one LLM call per query and can mislead on a private corpus the model knows nothing about. Decomposition costs five LLM calls and three retrievals where plain RAG costs one and one.
  • An LLM classifier routes by reasoning and pays a generation call per query; semantic routing compares embeddings with example questions and cannot say “none of these”. Guard it with a minimum score and a minimum margin, and hand unclear cases to the classifier.

Mental model

Everything in M18 happens before the first vector search or after the last one. The index from M12 to M17 stays as it is. A transformation multiplies the queries, so the stage after retrieval has to merge their results, and the merge decides which chunks survive the cut to the model.

Query–document asymmetry

Lesson 18.1: Query rewriting and expansion. Notebooks: 01a_query_expansion.ipynb, 01b_query_transformations_rag_techniques.ipynb (optional)

A retriever compares the question with each chunk and returns the closest. That works when the two look alike, and a question rarely looks like its answer. They differ in two ways.

Vocabulary. The user’s words are not the handbook’s words. In the M11 index, What happens if my server goes down? scores 0.000 against both chunks that answer it, because they say “outage”, “availability” and “incident” and never “down”. Its top three are reboot (0.416), sizing (0.389) and export (0.217), all matched on “server”. Embeddings (M14) close part of this gap, since they place paraphrases near each other.

Shape. A question is short and interrogative; a chunk is long, declarative and dense with terms. The 05a notebook’s example is a nine-word question, What is LangSmith, and why do we need it?, against a 400-token passage that answers it. Even with a good embedding model, the question’s vector sits closer to other questions than to the passage. The notebook calls this the query-document asymmetry.

Query (what the user typed) Chunk that answers it
What happens if my server goes down? Larkspur guarantees 99.95% monthly availability. If an outage pushes availability below that, you receive service credits on the next invoice.
How long does a refund take? Refunds go back to the original payment method and take 5 to 10 business days to appear on your statement.

The second pair shows the vocabulary gap at its smallest. TF-IDF does not stem, so “refund” in the question never meets “Refunds” in the chunk, and the timing chunk scores 0.000 too.

Rewriting and expansion

Lesson 18.1’s notebook closes the vocabulary gap with one LLM call. A prompt asks the model to “expand the following query to improve document retrieval by adding relevant synonyms, technical terms, and useful context”, and the expanded text goes to the retriever. The original question still goes to the answer prompt, so the model answers what the user asked, not the expansion.

The optional notebook (01b) shows the neighbouring moves on one example: rewriting a query to be more specific, a step-back query that is broader, and decomposition into two to four sub-queries. The 01a notebook lists the costs: one LLM call before every retrieval, and a dilution risk, because too many added terms pull the query toward everything.

Key Takeaways

  • A question rarely looks like its answer. It differs in vocabulary (the user’s words are not the handbook’s) and in shape (short and interrogative against long and declarative).
  • Expansion sends an LLM-enriched query to the retriever and the original question to the answer prompt. It costs one LLM call per retrieval, and too many added terms dilute the query.
  • Match the transformation to the gap: decompose multi-need questions, filter on facts, step back from narrow premises, use variants or HyDE for short or oddly worded ones. Plain retrieval is a valid choice.

RRF

Lesson 18.3: RAG-Fusion with Reciprocal Rank Fusion. Notebooks: 03a_rag_fusion.ipynb

Multi-query (lesson 18.2) asks an LLM for three phrasings of the question, retrieves for each, and merges the lists into a flat union. The union keeps every chunk once, in the order it was first seen, and throws away two signals: where each list ranked the chunk, and how many lists found it. A chunk that every variant ranked first looks the same as one that scraped in at fourth place in one list.

RAG-Fusion keeps both signals. It asks for five phrasings, because fusion needs several rankings before agreement means anything, and fuses the rankings with Reciprocal Rank Fusion. The 03a notebook scores each chunk as

score(d) = sum over the variants that retrieved d of 1 / (rank + k)

with rank counted from 0, as Python’s enumerate does, and k = 60. First place in one list adds 1/60 ≈ 0.0167 and fourth place adds 1/63 ≈ 0.0159, so being found by more variants matters more than placing first in any one of them. RRF uses ranks, never similarity scores, because scores from different queries are not on the same scale. M17’s fusion page covers the formula in depth, including the more common convention that counts ranks from 1; there the lists come from BM25 and dense retrieval, here from five phrasings of one question.

The outage question, fused

The interactive lab runs this on the M11 index: the same twelve Larkspur chunks, the same TF-IDF, and five hand-written variants per golden question in the style of the 03a prompt. Each variant retrieves its top 4, LangChain’s default k, minus chunks it shares no term with. For What happens if my server goes down? at k = 60:

# Chunk RRF score Ranks in variants 1 to 5 Golden set
1 reboot 0.0656 1, 2, 1, 0, none
2 sla 0.0500 0, 0, 0, none, none evidence
3 incidents 0.0331 none, 1, none, none, 0 evidence
4 sizing 0.0325 2, none, none, 1, none
5 export 0.0320 3, none, none, 2, none

The question as asked reaches neither evidence chunk. The variants reach both, because they use the handbook’s words: “outage”, “availability”, “incident”, “root-cause report”. At three chunks sent, recall is 1.00 against 0.00.

reboot still comes first. Four of the five variants match it, on “server” or on “does” (the chunk says “does not change”), and one variant drifted to How do I restart a server that has stopped responding?. RRF counts agreement, and agreement on a distractor counts too. Cut at two chunks and incidents is gone.

What k controls

A small k makes rank position dominate: at k = 1 first place is worth 1 and fourth place 0.25. A large k flattens the curve until only the number of votes counts. The 03a notebook runs k at 1, 5, 60 and 1000 and notes that the ordering only changes when rank and agreement disagree. In the refund question they do. refund-window is ranked 1 and 0 by two variants; retention is ranked 3, 3 and 2 by three. At k = 60 retention wins, 0.0479 to 0.0331. At k = 1 refund-window wins, 1.50 to 0.83.

Mean recall over the three golden questions, from the lab:

Chunks sent Question as asked (M11) Flat union RRF k = 1 RRF k = 5, 60 or 1000
2 0.50 0.83 0.83 0.67
3 0.50 0.83 1.00 1.00

Every merge beats the question as asked. Fusion and the union retrieve the same chunks and differ only in order, and the order decides what survives the cut. At three chunks every RRF setting beats the union, which leaves incidents at rank 5 because variant 1’s four chunks come first. At two chunks the conventional k = 60 loses to both the union and k = 1. Tune k and the cut on your own golden set; 60 is a convention.

Details that bite

  • Merge Documents, not ids. The 02a notebook’s original get_unique_union() returned doc.id values instead of documents, so the prompt’s context held bare identifiers and the model answered from memory, fluently. Its output showed one “document”. Key the merge by page_content, return the Document objects, and assert that the context holds real text.
  • Parse variants with a schema. Splitting the LLM’s reply on newlines turns headers, numbering and blank lines into queries. In RAG-Fusion each junk query produces a ranked list that votes in the fusion. Both notebooks bind a Pydantic model with with_structured_output() to get a guaranteed list[str].
  • Cost. One LLM call for the variants, then one retrieval per variant, run in parallel with retriever.map(). RRF itself is a dictionary and a sort.

Key Takeaways

  • RAG-Fusion retrieves for five phrasings and fuses the lists with Reciprocal Rank Fusion, which rewards chunks that many variants find. It uses ranks because scores from different queries are not comparable.
  • Agreement on a distractor counts too, and fusion only reorders what the variants retrieved. The order decides what survives the cut, so tune k and the number of chunks sent on your own golden set.
  • Merge Document objects keyed by page_content, never bare ids, and parse variants with a structured-output schema so junk lines do not become voting queries.

HyDE

Lesson 18.5: HyDE: embed a hypothetical answer. Notebooks: 05a_hyde.ipynb, 05b_hyde_walkthrough.ipynb (optional), 05c_hyde_rag_techniques.ipynb (optional)

HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings, Gao et al., arXiv:2212.10496) attacks the shape half of the query-document asymmetry. Instead of embedding the question, it asks an LLM to write an answer, embeds that answer, and searches with its vector. An invented answer is declarative, long and full of the vocabulary documentation uses, so it lands nearer the real passage than the question does.

The hypothetical answer is a search key. Its facts may be wrong, and that does not matter: it is discarded after embedding, never reaches the user and never enters the context. The chunks that come back are real.

Two models, one index

HyDE always needs an LLM to write and an embedding model to embed, and the embedding model must be the one that built the index, or the vectors are not comparable. The index itself is built plainly. Documents are already answer-shaped, and indexing through an LLM would cost a generation per chunk.

LangChain packages the three steps as HypotheticalDocumentEmbedder, which behaves differently for the two methods of the Embeddings interface:

Method What it does
embed_documents() Passes straight through to the base embedder, no LLM call
embed_query() Runs the LLM, then embeds the generated text

The 05a notebook checks the first row: a document embedded through the HyDE wrapper matches the plain embedding. That pass-through is what lets you hand the HyDE embedder to Chroma.from_documents() as a drop-in (indexing stays plain, every similarity_search() applies HyDE), or call embed_query() yourself and search an existing index with similarity_search_by_vector(). from_llm() takes a prompt key naming one of eight built-in templates inherited from the BEIR datasets the paper used; web_search is the general one, “Please write a passage to answer the question”. The 05a notebook writes its own prompt instead, asking for an excerpt from technical documentation, so the hypothetical answer matches the genre of the indexed chunks.

Several hypothetical answers

The paper generates several hypothetical answers and averages their embeddings, so no single invention dominates the search vector. The current, Runnable-based HypotheticalDocumentEmbedder cannot do this: its chain returns one string, so combine_embeddings() always averages exactly one vector. The 05a notebook generates three answers itself at temperature=0.8, embeds them and averages them, then checks their cosine similarity to each other. Near 1.0 means the three are near-identical and the average bought nothing for three times the cost.

When it helps and when it hurts

Plain retrieval HyDE
Cost per query one embedding call one LLM call plus one embedding call
Short or vague questions often misses the LLM supplies the missing vocabulary
Niche or private corpus retrieves what is there the LLM invents plausible jargon and can steer away from the right chunk
Questions that already read like documents strong no better, sometimes worse

The Larkspur handbook is the private case. No model was trained on Larkspur’s refund window or its outage credits, so a hypothetical answer to What happens if my server goes down? is written from what the model knows about hosting providers in general. That text is likely to use outage vocabulary, which helps here, and it can also name terms the handbook never uses, which pull the vector toward nothing. The 05a notebook ends with the test that settles it: retrieve with and without HyDE on the same questions and count the overlap, rather than assume a gain.

Key Takeaways

  • HyDE embeds an LLM-written answer instead of the question, because an answer-shaped text lands nearer the real passage. The invented answer is only a search key and is discarded.
  • Embed with the same model that built the index. HypotheticalDocumentEmbedder keeps indexing plain and applies the LLM only in embed_query().
  • HyDE helps short or vague questions and can hurt on a private corpus, where the LLM invents jargon the documents never use. Compare retrieval with and without it before turning it on.

Decomposing compound questions

Lesson 18.6: Query decomposition for multi-hop questions. Notebooks: 06a_query_decomposition.ipynb, 06b_query_decomposition_walkthrough.ipynb (optional)

Some questions carry two or three needs. What is LangSmith, and why do we need it?, the 06a notebook’s example, asks for a definition and a justification, and the answers live in different chunks. Dense retrieval gives the whole question one vector, which lands between the two needs and close to neither. With a top-k cut, one need can crowd the other out entirely.

Decomposition splits the question before retrieval and answers each part on its own:

The synthesis prompt sees the three question and answer pairs, never the retrieved chunks, so its context stays small however many sub-questions there were. The 06a notebook splits its corpus into 100-token chunks so that each holds about one idea, which makes the gain visible: it counts the unique chunks the three sub-questions reach against the single query’s.

What it costs

Step LLM calls Retrievals
Decompose 1 0
Answer each sub-question 3 3
Synthesize 1 0
Total 5 3

Plain RAG costs one and one, so decomposition is five times the LLM calls for one user question, the most expensive technique in M18. The sub-questions are independent, so rag_chain.batch(...) runs them in parallel and recovers most of the wall-clock time at the same token cost. Errors propagate: a bad sub-question produces a confident wrong sub-answer, and the synthesis has no chunks to check it against.

Get the list right

The first version of the notebook asked for prose and split it on newlines. The prompt asked for sub-questions and then for “multiple search queries”, the model returned both under markdown headers, and the split produced about 18 items: headers, blank strings and bullets. Each one triggered a retrieval and an LLM call, roughly 18 of each instead of 3, and the same chain returned 3 clean lines on some runs, so the bug came and went. The fix is a prompt that asks for one thing and a Pydantic schema bound with with_structured_output(), which returns a list[str] of exactly the sub-questions.

On the Larkspur handbook

M11’s symptom table sends “half an answer; one fact found, another missed” to decomposition. Can I export my data after I cancel my plan? reads like two needs, how to export and what happens to data after cancelling. The M11 lab already retrieves both evidence chunks for it at k = 2, because one chunk says “Export your data” and the other “After a plan is cancelled”. Decomposing it would cost four extra LLM calls and add nothing. Decompose the questions whose parts the single query misses, found on the golden set, and route the rest past it (lesson 18.7).

Key Takeaways

  • One vector for a compound question lands between its needs and close to neither. Decomposition gives each sub-question its own retrieval and answer, then synthesises from the question and answer pairs.
  • Three sub-questions cost five LLM calls and three retrievals. Run them in parallel to recover wall-clock time, and remember that a bad sub-answer reaches the synthesis unchecked.
  • Get the sub-question list from a structured-output schema, and decompose only the questions whose parts a single query misses on the golden set.

Logical vs semantic routing

Lesson 18.7: Routing: LLM classifier and semantic routing. Notebooks: 07a_routing_with_llm_classifier.ipynb, 07b_semantic_routing.ipynb

A router decides where a question goes before anything answers it: which prompt, which data source, which transformation. Lesson 18.7 builds the same four-way router twice, over the notebooks’ personal finance, book review, health and fitness, and travel destinations. Logical routing asks an LLM to read the question and choose. Semantic routing embeds the question and picks the destination whose example questions it is closest to.

Logical (LLM classifier) Semantic (embedding similarity)
Decides by the model reasoning over the question cosine similarity to each route’s example questions
Cost per query one LLM call one embedding call, about 100 times cheaper in the 07a notebook’s comparison
Handles nuance, new phrasings, questions with several clauses questions that resemble the examples
Fails by picking a plausible but wrong route routing on surface resemblance; a tie looks like a decision
Out-of-scope questions still routed unless it has an “other” route always routed: there is no “none of these”

The LLM classifier

The first version of the 07a notebook asked for the category as free text and compared it with exact strings. The model answered 'health & fitness' and 'travel guides', which match neither 'Health & Fitness' nor 'Travel Guide', so every branch fell through and the router returned None. The notebook still appeared to work. The fix binds a Pydantic model with with_structured_output() whose destination field is a Literal of the four route names, so no other value can come back, plus a one-sentence reasoning field that makes each decision readable in traces.

A Literal guarantees a valid route, not a sensible one. Asked What is the capital of France?, the router still picks one of the four. Production routers need an explicit other destination, a regression set of question to expected route (adding a route changes decisions on old questions), and usually a smaller, cheaper model for routing than for answering.

Semantic routing

Each route is defined by a few example questions, three per route in the 07b notebook, embedded once at start-up. Per query the router embeds the question, takes each route’s highest cosine similarity to its examples, and picks the highest route. The routing path never calls an LLM.

The 07b notebook shows why the winner alone is not enough (scores from text-embedding-3-small):

  • How can I travel on a budget? is one of the travel examples word for word and scores 1.0000, while personal finance still scores about 0.50 on “budget”.
  • Are meal-prep books worth buying? wins by a margin of about 0.002, with every route near 0.28, and book review is not even in the top two despite the word “books”. The router returns that answer with the same confidence as the exact match.

Two thresholds turn the scores into a confidence check: a minimum top score (did anything match?) and a minimum margin over the runner-up (was the choice distinguishable?). The notebook uses 0.35 and 0.02 and calls them illustrative; tune them on logged traffic. Questions that fail either go to the LLM classifier, which makes the two routers one system:

Most traffic pays for one embedding; only the unclear questions pay for a generation.

Larkspur’s routes

A Larkspur support assistant could route on the handbook’s own sections. The examples below are written for illustration; the notebooks’ scores do not transfer to them.

Route Handbook chunks behind it Example questions
billing billing, cancel, refund-window, refund-timing, promo How long does a refund take? When am I charged for an annual plan?

Do I get a refund if an outage lasts a day? belongs to billing and reliability at once. Key Takeaways

  • Logical routing asks an LLM to choose; semantic routing picks the route whose example questions are closest by embedding, about 100 times cheaper per query in the notebook’s comparison.
  • A Literal output field guarantees a valid route name, not a sensible one. Add an explicit other route and a regression set of questions with expected routes.
  • Gate semantic routes on a minimum top score and a minimum margin over the runner-up, and send failures to the LLM classifier. Tune both thresholds on logged traffic.

Interview Spotlight: 5 Questions You Might Get Asked

Production questions of the kind asked in applied AI engineering interviews.

1. You ship RAG-Fusion for Larkspur’s support assistant. A customer asks “What happens if my server goes down?” and gets reboot instructions instead of the SLA credit policy. Recall on the golden set went up. What happened?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Prints the fused list with each chunk’s votes and ranks: the reboot chunk can win because four of five variants matched “server” or “does”, which is agreement on a distractor
  • Checks the variants themselves; a variant that drifts (“How do I restart a server…”) adds a vote for the wrong chunk
  • Looks at the cut: at three chunks both evidence chunks are in, at two only sla is, so the number of chunks sent matters as much as the fusion
  • Fixes with a reranker over the fused pool (M19), a lower k or fewer drifting variants, and adds the question to the golden set at the production cut

2. Your latency budget is 1.5 s and a plain retrieval takes 300 ms. Product wants multi-query, HyDE and decomposition “on every question”. What do you propose?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Counts the calls: multi-query adds one LLM call and N retrievals, HyDE one LLM call, decomposition five LLM calls and three retrievals where plain RAG has one and one
  • Routes first: send compound questions to decomposition, short or vague ones to variants or HyDE, and direct lookups straight to retrieval
  • Runs the per-variant retrievals and sub-questions in parallel (retriever.map(), .batch()), and uses a small model for rewriting
  • Measures each technique per question type against the M11 baseline and keeps only the ones that win where they are applied

3. How do you show that RAG-Fusion helped, rather than only cost more?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Compares against the plain pipeline on the same frozen golden set, at the cut the product really uses
  • Reports recall at that cut, answer quality, latency and LLM cost per question, and breaks results down by question type
  • Watches regressions on direct lookups: in the lab, RRF at k = 60 and two chunks drops the refund question from 1.00 (union) to 0.50
  • Logs the variants and fused lists in production so a bad variant or a distractor that keeps winning shows up in traces

4. Larkspur routes questions semantically to billing, data, reliability and servers. “Do I get a refund if an outage lasts a day?” goes to servers. How do you make the router safer?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Reads all route scores and the margin, not only the winner; a question that straddles billing and reliability will have close scores
  • Adds a minimum top score and a minimum margin, tuned on logged traffic, and sends anything below either to an LLM classifier or a default prompt
  • Gives the classifier an explicit “other” destination, because a Literal guarantees a valid route, not a sensible one
  • Adds exemplars for the cross-topic phrasings that fail, and keeps a regression set of query to expected route

5. A self-querying retriever turns “annual-plan refund rules updated after 2025” into a metadata filter. Some days it returns nothing, and the logs show no error. What do you check?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Logs the structured query each call produced, because the query constructor is an LLM and its output varies between runs
  • Looks for a filter value of the wrong type (“2025” as a string), which a store such as Chroma rejects for $gt, and coerces numeric values before they reach the store
  • Checks the metadata itself: an attribute that is not declared cannot be filtered, and “annual” against “Annual” breaks equality
  • Treats an empty result after a filter as a signal to retry without the filter or to ask the user, not as “nothing matched”

Key Takeaways

  • Print what the retriever saw before you change the prompt: the original query, every variant and every list. Fluent answers can come from an empty context, as the 02a notebook’s merge bug showed.
  • Match the transformation to the gap. Vocabulary gaps want variants or expansion, shape gaps want HyDE, compound questions want decomposition, facts the embedding cannot compare want a metadata filter.
  • RRF rewards agreement, and agreement on a distractor still counts. Tune k and the cut on your golden set instead of taking 60 on trust.
  • Route cheaply by similarity, check the margin, and pay for an LLM classifier only when the margin is small.
