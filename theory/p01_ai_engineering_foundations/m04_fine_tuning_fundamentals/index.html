M04 Fine-tuning fundamentals

Fine-tuning changes a model’s weights with your own examples, so the change travels with the model instead of sitting in every prompt. It is good at behaviour (a format, a tone, a refusal, one narrow task) and poor at facts that change. The M04 notebooks build on four ideas: how instruction data turns a text predictor into an assistant, how that data becomes token tensors, what the Hugging Face Trainer does with them, and how to tell whether the result is better.

Open the lab

The notebooks fine-tune EleutherAI/pythia-70m on lamini_docs, 1,400 questions and answers about one company’s product. These pages run the same steps on the Larkspur Cloud support assistant from M11 to M13: the support team wants answers that lead with Yes or No, state the policy in a sentence, and decline questions that are not about Larkspur. The examples reuse M11’s golden questions (How long does a refund take?, Can I export my data after I cancel my plan?, What happens if my server goes down?) and add the notebooks’ off-topic probe, What do you think of Mars?

TL;DR

  • Fine-tune for behaviour, retrieve for facts. The notebooks’ clearest result is a format change: the base Llama 2 continues “Tell me how to train my dog to sit” with more questions, and the chat model answers it. A fact that changes next month belongs in the M11 index.
  • The training text is all the model learns from. The lamini/lamini_docs copy that lesson 4.5 trains on joins each question to its answer with no template and no end token, in all 1,260 training rows. A model trained that way never learns where an answer stops.
  • Truncation and padding decide what survives. In the lab, max_length = 48 cuts 11 answer tokens from the refund example with right truncation, or the header and 7 question tokens with left truncation. Padding the batch to its longest row adds 18 pad tokens, 13% of the batch.
  • Count examples, not steps. The lesson 4.5 run is max_steps = 3 with a batch of 1 and 4 accumulation steps: 12 of 1,260 training examples, under 1% of one epoch, and the first update runs at learning rate 0.
  • Exact match fails on free text. The notebooks’ evaluation set has 0 exact matches in 139 answers, and 127 predicted answers repeat a sentence. Measure the behaviour you trained for, on held-out questions, against the prompted or RAG baseline.

Mental model

When evaluation shows a problem, the fix is usually in the examples or in how they were prepared (a missing end token, a truncated answer, a template the model never sees at inference), and the loop goes back to the data before it goes back to the training settings.

When to fine-tune

Lesson 4.1: Why fine-tune. Notebooks: 01a_why_finetuning_lab.ipynb, 01b_why_finetuning_lecture.ipynb (optional)

Fine-tuning updates the model’s weights with your examples. Prompting changes only the input, and RAG (M11) changes only what the input contains. Each fixes a different gap:

Prompting RAG Fine-tuning
What changes The instructions and examples in each call The evidence pasted into each call The weights
Good for Format and tone you can describe in a few lines Facts that change, private documents, citations Behaviour too long or subtle to describe: a house style, a narrow task, consistent refusals
Cost of a change Edit the prompt Re-index a document Rebuild the dataset and retrain
Per-call cost Grows with every instruction and example Retrieval plus a few chunks Lowest: the behaviour is in the weights
Main risk Long prompts, drift between calls Retrieval misses the evidence Stale facts, lost general ability, no citations

For the Larkspur support assistant the split is clear. How long does a refund take? has an answer that the policy team will change, so it stays in the index. Leading every answer with Yes or No and declining What do you think of Mars? is behaviour, and a fine-tune can carry it on a smaller, cheaper model. Most production systems that fine-tune also retrieve.

If you cannot write a few hundred examples of the behaviour you want, you cannot fine-tune for it, and you cannot evaluate it either.

Check yourself

1. Why does the refund-time answer stay in the Larkspur index while opening every answer with Yes or No can go into a fine-tune?

The refund time is a fact the policy team will change: RAG picks up a change with a re-index, while a fine-tuned model would need a rebuilt dataset and a retrain, and risks stale facts. Leading with Yes or No, and declining off-topic questions, is behaviour, which a fine-tune can carry in the weights on a smaller, cheaper model.

2. You send Tell me how to train my dog to sit, without any wrapper, to the Llama 2 base and chat models. What does each return, and why?

The base model continues with similar questions (“Tell me how to train my dog to stay. …”), because pre-training taught it to continue text. The chat model treats it as a request but first finishes the sentence (“on command.”), because it was trained with the [INST] wrapper; with the wrapper its answer starts cleanly.

3. A team wants to fine-tune for a behaviour but cannot write a few hundred examples of it. What should they do?

Not fine-tune: without the examples they can neither train for the behaviour nor evaluate it. The decision chain says to prompt it if a clear prompt with examples works (M05), retrieve it if the gap is a fact that changes (M11), and otherwise define the task before training.

Pre-training, then fine-tuning

Lesson 4.2: Where fine-tuning fits. Notebooks: 02a_where_finetuning_fits_in_lab.ipynb (optional), 02b_where_finetuning_fits_in_lecture.ipynb (optional)

Both stages train the same way: show the model text and adjust the weights so it predicts each next token better. What differs is the data.

The lesson 4.2 notebook prints both kinds side by side. The first rows it streams from C4 (load_dataset("c4", "en", streaming=True)) are scraped web pages: whatever text was on the page, in whatever order. The fine-tuning file, lamini_docs.jsonl, has 1,400 rows, each a question and an answer about one product. Pre-training gives the model language and general knowledge from a huge, unlabelled corpus; fine-tuning shows it, in a small labelled one, what a good response looks like.

Pre-training Fine-tuning
Data Raw text, unlabelled, web scale Curated pairs, labelled, hundreds to thousands of rows (1,400 here)
What the model learns Language, facts, patterns of text A format, a task, a tone, when to decline
Who runs it Model labs, once per model You, whenever the behaviour needs to change

The notebook then formats the same rows two ways, and both are common:

  • Text only: one text field holding the whole example, ### Question: and ### Answer: included. Trainers that take a text_column, such as AutoTrain in lesson 4.7, expect this.
  • Question and answer: the prompt in one field (### Question: up to ### Answer:) and the answer in another. Keeping them apart lets you score only the answer at evaluation time, and mask the prompt out of the loss if you choose.

Both are written to JSON Lines, one example per line, which is what datasets.load_dataset("json", …) reads in lesson 4.4.

A Larkspur example in the second form:

{"question": "### Question:\nCan I export my data after I cancel my plan?\n\n### Answer:", "answer": "Yes, for 30 days. After that the data is permanently deleted, so export it from Settings, then Data, first."}

Check yourself

1. Pre-training and fine-tuning both train the model to predict each next token. What makes their results differ?

The data. Pre-training uses a huge, unlabelled corpus of raw web text such as C4, and gives the model language, facts and patterns of text. Fine-tuning uses a small, curated, labelled set (1,400 question and answer rows in lamini_docs.jsonl) and teaches a format, a task, a tone and when to decline.

2. You will train the Larkspur examples with AutoTrain, and at evaluation time you want to score only the answer. Which of the two formats does each step need?

AutoTrain takes a text_column, so training needs the text-only form: one text field with ### Question: and ### Answer: inside it. Scoring only the answer needs the question-and-answer form, with the prompt and the answer in separate fields. The notebook formats the same rows both ways and writes both to JSON Lines.

3. A teammate proposes pre-training a model on the Larkspur handbook to teach it the house style. Why does the comparison point to fine-tuning instead?

Pre-training needs web-scale raw text and is run by model labs once per model; it teaches language and general knowledge. Fine-tuning needs hundreds to thousands of curated pairs, you run it whenever the behaviour needs to change, and a tone or format is what it teaches.

Instruction tuning

Lesson 4.3: Instruction tuning. Notebooks: 03a_instruction_tuning_lab.ipynb, 03b_instruction_tuning_lecture.ipynb (optional)

Instruction tuning is fine-tuning on pairs of a request and a good response. It is the first step that turned the base Llama 2 of lesson 4.1 into the chat model. What it teaches is a pattern: text shaped like a request is followed by text shaped like an answer, and then the answer ends.

The lesson 4.3 notebook streams the first five rows of tatsu-lab/alpaca, an instruction dataset where every row has three fields: instruction (the request), input (optional context, empty in all five rows shown) and output (the answer). A prompt template turns each row into training text, and the notebook keeps two:

Below is an instruction that describes a task, paired with an input that provides further context. Write a response that appropriately completes the request.

### Instruction:
{instruction}

### Input:
{input}

### Response:

The result is written to alpaca_processed.jsonl with one {"input": …, "output": …} object per line. The same shape works for Larkspur: the instruction is the customer’s question, the input is empty, and the output is an answer in the house style.

Any difference between the two answers comes from the training rows alone. The notebook ships without saved outputs, so run it to see them.

The template at inference

A model learns the exact surface form of its training text, so the prompt at inference has to match it. Lesson 4.1 showed the cost: the Llama 2 chat model, given a request without its [INST] wrapper, first finished the sentence (“on command.”) before answering.

The M04 notebooks are a useful warning. Lesson 4.4 builds ### Question: / ### Answer: prompts, but the lamini/lamini_docs copy that lesson 4.5 trains on carries none: in all 1,260 training rows input_ids is the bare question followed immediately by the bare answer (…from Lamini models?There are several metrics…), with no separator and no end token. The inference helper then sends the bare question, which matches that training text, so the two stay consistent by accident. Change either side alone and the model sees a format it was never trained on.

Three rules keep training and inference in step:

  • One template, in code, used by both. Write the formatting function once and import it in the data script and the serving code.
  • Mark where the answer starts and where it ends. A header such as ### Answer: tells the model to start answering; an end token (<|endoftext|> for Pythia) tells it, and your decoder, that the answer is over.
  • Check the tokenized row, not the string. Decode a few prepared rows before training; the lab in lesson 4.4 shows what to look for.

Data preparation

Lesson 4.4: Data preparation. Notebooks: 04a_data_preparation_lab.ipynb, 04b_data_preparation_lecture.ipynb (optional)

A model trains on token ids, in rectangular batches. Data preparation turns each formatted example into one row of ids, makes the rows fit a length limit and a common width, and holds some rows back for evaluation.

Tokenize. The notebook loads the EleutherAI/pythia-70m tokenizer, the one its model was trained with. "Hi, how are you?" becomes six ids, [12764, 13, 849, 403, 368, 32], and decode turns them back into the same string. Text and tokens are different units: the first lamini_docs example is 358 characters and 77 tokens.

Pad. A batch is one tensor, so its rows need one width. Pythia’s tokenizer has its own <|padding|> token, but the notebook sets tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token, so padding uses id 0, the end-of-text id. With padding=True, ["Hi, how are you?", "I'm good", "Yes"] (6, 3 and 1 tokens) become three rows of 6, the short ones filled with 0 on the right. attention_mask marks real tokens with 1 and padding with 0, so attention skips the padding.

Truncate. max_length with truncation=True caps every row. The default cuts from the right; the notebook switches to truncation_side = "left". At max_length=3 the first sentence keeps [12764, 13, 849] (“Hi, how”) from the right cut and [403, 368, 32] (“ are you?”) from the left.

Label and split. For a causal language model the target is the input itself, shifted by one inside the model, so the notebook adds labels as a copy of input_ids. train_test_split(test_size=0.1, shuffle=True, seed=123) then turns the 1,400 rows into 1,260 for training and 140 held out; the fixed seed gives the same split on every run.

Each of these choices changes what the model learns, and three of them fail silently:

  • Right truncation cuts the end of the answer, and the end token with it. The model trains on answers that stop mid-sentence.
  • Left truncation cuts the start: the ### Question: header first, then the question. The model trains on answers to questions it cannot see.
  • No end token. The Pythia tokenizer appends nothing, so a question-plus-answer row never shows the model where an answer stops. Append tokenizer.eos_token to each training text. Since padding reuses id 0 and labels copies it, set padded positions in labels to -100 so the loss ignores them.

The interactive lab builds a three-example Larkspur batch with the real Pythia tokens. At max_length = 48 right truncation cuts 11 answer tokens from the refund example, and left truncation cuts the header and 7 question tokens from it instead. Padding to the longest row adds 18 pad tokens, 13% of the batch; padding to a fixed max_length of 64 with the end token appended adds 52, 27%. No row ends with <|endoftext|> until you append it.

In the notebook itself the limit never bites. Its tokenize_function caps rows at 2,048 tokens, and the longest of the 1,400 lamini_docs rows is 479 (median 75, 90th percentile 128). Measure your own length distribution before choosing max_length: set it just above the longest rows you want to keep whole, and decide on purpose what happens to the rest.

Two notebook details are worth knowing before you reuse the code. tokenize_function reads only item [0] of each batch it receives, so it works only because map is called with batch_size=1. And with one example per batch there is nothing to pad: the training run in lesson 4.5 never pads at all.

Checking the rows

Tokenizing does not check the data. Read the rows before you train on them; in lamini_docs.jsonl a short pass finds:

Check What it finds in the 1,400 rows Why it matters
Empty rows 1 row with a blank question and a blank answer It tokenizes to zero ids; lesson 4.5’s custom Trainer has a guard that skips empty inputs
Duplicate questions 3 extra copies of questions already in the file Duplicates weigh a behaviour twice, and one copy can land in the test split while the other trains
Refusals 39 answers that ask to “keep the discussion relevant to Lamini” These rows are what teach the model to decline off-topic questions such as What do you think of Mars?
Length 0 rows over 2,048 tokens; the longest is 479 Sets max_length, and with it memory and step time

The refusal rows show how a behaviour gets in: the model declines off-topic questions because 39 training answers do. A Larkspur dataset needs its own share of those rows, written in the house style, or the fine-tuned assistant will answer What do you think of Mars? as cheerfully as the base model. Deduplicate before you split, and check that no test question also appears in training.

Check yourself

1. The Pythia tokenizer appends no end token, and padding reuses the end-of-text id. What do you add to each training text, and what do you set at padded label positions?

Append tokenizer.eos_token to each training text, so every row shows the model where an answer stops. Then set the padded positions in labels to -100: padding uses id 0 and labels copies input_ids, so without that step the loss would train on the padding.

2. In the lab's Larkspur batch at max_length 48, what does each truncation side remove from the refund example?

Right truncation cuts 11 answer tokens from the end, so the model trains on an answer that stops mid-sentence. Left truncation keeps the answer but cuts the ### Question: header and 7 question tokens, so the model trains on an answer to a question it cannot fully see. Neither raises an error.

3. A fine-tuned Larkspur assistant answers What do you think of Mars? as cheerfully as the base model. What was missing from its training data?

Refusal rows. In lamini_docs.jsonl the model learns to decline off-topic questions because 39 training answers ask to keep the discussion relevant to Lamini. A Larkspur dataset needs its own share of those rows, written in the house style.

Trainer

Lesson 4.5: Training. Notebooks: 05a_training_lab.ipynb, 05b_training_lecture.ipynb (optional)

The Hugging Face Trainer runs the training loop you would otherwise write by hand: batch the data, run the model forward, compute the loss, run it backward, step the optimizer and the learning-rate schedule, evaluate, log and save checkpoints. TrainingArguments holds every setting. The lesson 4.5 notebook trains EleutherAI/pythia-70m (70.4 million parameters, about 0.28 GB of weights in 32-bit floats) on lamini/lamini_docs with these:

Argument Value What it does
learning_rate 1.0e-5 Peak step size, reached after warmup
max_steps 3 Optimizer updates to run; overrides num_train_epochs
per_device_train_batch_size 1 Examples per forward pass
gradient_accumulation_steps 4 Forward passes summed into one update
warmup_steps 1 Updates spent ramping the learning rate up from 0
optim "adafactor" An optimizer that stores less state per weight than Adam
eval_steps, save_steps 120 Evaluate and checkpoint every 120 updates
logging_steps 1 Log after every update
load_best_model_at_end True Reload the checkpoint with the lowest eval_loss when training ends

One optimizer step, with a batch of 1 and four accumulation steps:

Gradient accumulation gives the update of a batch of 4 while holding only one example in memory. After the gradients are zeroed, the next step starts again from a fresh micro-batch.

The notebook does not use transformers.Trainer directly. It imports a subclass from its utilities.py that adds three things: it skips a batch whose input_ids are empty (the blank row lesson 4.4 found), it logs iteration time, FLOPs per second and remaining time, and it smooths the logged loss. That smoothing is an exponential average that gives each new step a weight of 1/100, so in a 3-step run the logged loss barely moves from the first value. Read the raw loss when you judge a short run.

The FLOPs figure uses the rule Trainer.floating_point_ops implements: 6 × tokens × non-embedding parameters. Pythia-70m has 44,670,976 non-embedding parameters, so the notebook’s estimate for a 2,048-token row times 4 accumulation steps is about 2.2 trillion operations per update.

On the transformers 5.17 this course pins, the notebook no longer runs as written. TrainingArguments rejects evaluation_strategy (now eval_strategy); the model has no floating_point_ops method (the Trainer does); and the subclass is written against an older Trainer: it passes arguments to __init__ by position, which no longer lines up, and its training_step lacks the num_items_in_batch argument the loop now passes.

Steps, examples and epochs

A step is one optimizer update, not one example. The arithmetic for the lesson 4.5 run:

Value
Examples per update 1 per batch × 4 accumulated = 4
Updates max_steps = 3
Examples seen 12
Training rows 1,260
Share of one epoch 0.95%

The learning rate is not constant either. With the default linear schedule, warmup_steps=1 and 3 steps in all, transformers’ get_linear_schedule_with_warmup gives the three updates learning rates of 0, 1e-5 and 5e-6. The first of the three updates changes nothing. And because evaluation and checkpoints happen every 120 updates, this run never computes an eval_loss, so load_best_model_at_end has no checkpoint to choose.

That is why the notebook calls its result “finetuned slightly” and then loads lamini/lamini_docs_finetuned, the same base model trained for two epochs: 2,520 examples against 12. A run that sees under 1% of the data, with one of its three updates at a learning rate of 0, shows the mechanics of training and almost none of its effect.

For a real Larkspur run, size it in examples: decide how many epochs the data supports, derive the number of updates, set warmup to a fraction of them, and put eval_steps well inside the run so the held-out loss is tracked while it trains.

Check yourself

1. With a batch size of 1 and 4 gradient-accumulation steps, how does the trainer produce one optimizer update?

It runs four micro-batches of one example each forward and backward, adding their gradients. Once four are collected it steps the optimizer at the learning rate from the schedule and zeroes the gradients. The update matches a batch of 4 while only one example is held in memory.

2. In the lesson 4.5 run, what learning rates do the three updates get, and what share of an epoch does the model see?

0, 1e-5 and 5e-6, from the linear schedule with warmup_steps=1, so the first update changes nothing. Three updates of 4 examples each see 12 of the 1,260 training rows: 0.95% of one epoch.

3. Why does load_best_model_at_end have no checkpoint to choose in the lesson 4.5 run, and how should a real Larkspur run be set?

Evaluation and checkpoints happen every 120 updates and the run has 3, so it never computes an eval_loss. Size a real run in examples: decide the epochs the data supports, derive the number of updates, set warmup to a fraction of them, and put eval_steps well inside the run.

Evaluation

Lesson 4.6: Evaluation. Notebooks: 06a_evaluation_lab.ipynb, 06b_evaluation_lecture.ipynb (optional)

Training loss tells you the model fits its training rows. Evaluation asks whether it does the job on rows it never saw, and whether it still does the jobs it could do before. The lesson 4.6 notebook tries three methods on lamini/lamini_docs_finetuned, from strictest to broadest.

Exact match. is_exact_match(a, b) strips both strings and compares them. The notebook generates answers for the test split and counts matches. Two details limit what that count can say:

  • The loop breaks once i > n with n = 10, so it scores 12 of the 140 test questions.
  • inference calls generate with max_length=100, a limit on prompt plus answer. For 42 of the 140 test pairs, question and target answer together are longer than 100 tokens, 3 of them among the 12 scored, so the model could not produce the target even if it knew it word for word.

A stored evaluation set. lamini/lamini_docs_evaluation holds 139 predicted answers next to their targets. Scoring it the notebook’s way gives 0 exact matches in 139. The failure is visible in the text: 127 of the 139 predictions repeat a sentence of more than 20 characters, and the median prediction is 5,128 characters against a median target of 304. The first one says “Yes, Lamini can generate technical documentation or user manuals for software projects” and then repeats “Additionally, Lamini can be trained on specific technical questions…” until it is cut off. The lamini/lamini_docs training rows carry no end token (lesson 4.4), so the model has no example of an answer stopping.

A benchmark. The last cell runs ARC Easy through EleutherAI’s lm-evaluation-harness. A benchmark of general questions does not measure the fine-tuned task; it checks that fine-tuning did not cost the model what it could already do. Run it on the base and the fine-tuned model and compare. The cell calls lm-evaluation-harness/main.py, a checkout the course does not include, so install the harness first.

Pick the method by what a correct answer looks like:

Most fine-tunes need more than one. For the Larkspur assistant, whether an answer opens with Yes or No is a string check; whether its policy sentence matches the handbook needs a judge or a reviewer; and a benchmark run before and after guards general ability. Whatever the metric, read the failures by hand: the repetition above shows up in the first prediction, long before any score.

LoRA and 4-bit loading

Lesson 4.7: Fine-tuning Llama 2 with AutoTrain. Notebooks: 07a_3_ways_fine_tuning_llama_2_with_autotrain.ipynb (optional)

Lessons 4.3 to 4.6 update every weight of a 70-million-parameter model. That does not scale to a 7-billion-parameter one on a single GPU. Llama 2 7B has 6,738,415,616 parameters: 13.5 GB in 16-bit floats before any gradients or optimizer state. The lesson 4.7 notebook fine-tunes it with two flags that change what is stored and what is trained, --use_peft and --use_int4.

LoRA (low-rank adaptation, the method behind PEFT here) freezes every original weight matrix W and trains two small matrices beside it, A and B, whose product is added to the layer’s output. With rank r, a layer of shape d_out × d_in gains r × (d_in + d_out) trainable parameters:

For the attention query projection of Llama 2 7B at rank 16, that is 131,072 trainable parameters beside 16,777,216 frozen ones, 0.78%. Across the model, with the settings AutoTrain uses by default (lora_r 16, target_modules "all-linear"):

Setup Trainable parameters Share of the model
Full fine-tuning 6,738,415,616 100%
LoRA, rank 16, every linear layer 39,976,960 0.59%
LoRA, rank 16, query and value projections only 8,388,608 0.12%

These counts come from peft 0.20 wrapping a Llama 2 7B-shaped model built on PyTorch’s meta device, so no weights were downloaded; they match r × (d_in + d_out) summed over the targeted layers.

4-bit loading stores the frozen weights in 4 bits instead of 16, about 3.4 GB for 6.74 billion weights before quantization overhead, while the LoRA matrices train in higher precision. Combined, this is QLoRA, one of the “3 ways” the notebook’s title refers to. Gradients and optimizer state exist only for the 0.59%, which is what brings a 7B fine-tune within reach of one GPU.

The adapter is saved on its own: 39,976,960 parameters, about 80 MB in 16-bit floats. You can merge it into the base model for serving, or keep one base model and load a different adapter per task.

The AutoTrain command today

The notebook’s command is one CLI call:

autotrain llm --train --project_name "llama2-autotrain-openassitant" \
  --model TinyPixel/Llama-2-7B-bf16-sharded --data_path timdettmers/openassistant-guanaco \
  --text_column text --use_peft --use_int4 --learning_rate 0.4 --train_batch_size 3 \
  --num_train_epochs 2 --trainer sft --model_max_length 1048 --block_size 1048 \
  --push_to_hub --repo_id trojrobert/llama2-autotrain-openassistant > training.log

It reads a text column in which each row holds a whole conversation (the text-only format from lesson 4.2), trains with supervised fine-tuning, and pushes the result to the Hugging Face Hub. Two things to know before you run it:

  • The flag names are from an older AutoTrain. In autotrain-advanced 0.8.36, the arguments are generated from its LLMTrainingParams: --peft, --quantization int4 (the default), --lr, --batch-size, --epochs, and --username with --push-to-hub. The CLI parses arguments strictly, so --use_peft, --use_int4, --learning_rate, --train_batch_size, --num_train_epochs and --repo_id stop it with an error.
  • The learning rate is far too high. 0.4 is more than 13,000 times AutoTrain’s default of 3e-5, and 40,000 times lesson 4.5’s 1e-5. Keep the default unless a measured run says otherwise.

Its other defaults are worth reading too: 4 gradient-accumulation steps, warmup over 10% of the run, a 2,048-token model_max_length, and an add_eos_token setting, on by default, that appends the end token in its default trainer, the step lessons 4.4 and 4.6 show the M04 data missing.

The notebook’s other two cells sketch Lamini’s BasicModelRunner and trl’s SFTTrainer, but use names the notebook never defines (base_model, peft_config, training_arguments, among others), so they do not run as written.

Check yourself

1. Why does LoRA with 4-bit loading bring a 7B fine-tune within reach of one GPU?

LoRA freezes every original weight matrix and trains small A and B matrices beside it, so at rank 16 on every linear layer only 39,976,960 parameters (0.59%) train, and gradients and optimizer state exist only for those. 4-bit loading stores the frozen weights in about 3.4 GB instead of 13.5 GB in 16-bit floats. Together this is QLoRA.

2. How many trainable parameters does rank-16 LoRA add to a 4096 by 4096 query projection, and what share of the layer is that?

r × (d_in + d_out) = 16 × (4,096 + 4,096) = 131,072 trainable parameters, beside 16,777,216 frozen ones: 0.78%.

3. You run the notebook's autotrain llm command on autotrain-advanced 0.8.36 and it stops with an error. Why, and what else would you change?

Its flag names are from an older AutoTrain, and the CLI parses arguments strictly. Use --peft, --quantization int4, --lr, --batch-size, --epochs, and --username with --push-to-hub. Also drop the learning rate of 0.4, which is more than 13,000 times the default 3e-5; keep the default unless a measured run says otherwise.

Interview Spotlight: 5 Questions You Might Get Asked

Production questions in the style of OpenAI, Anthropic and Google interviews for applied AI roles.

1. Larkspur fine-tuned a small model on 2,000 support transcripts. In testing it answers the refund question correctly, then keeps going: it repeats the policy sentence until the response hits its token limit. What do you check?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Decodes a few training rows and checks that each ends with the tokenizer’s end-of-text token; the Pythia tokenizer does not append one, so a model trained on raw question-plus-answer text never sees an answer end
  • Checks that truncation did not cut the end token off long examples, and that padding with the same id is masked to -100 in labels
  • Confirms generation stops on that token (eos_token_id) and sets max_new_tokens rather than a total max_length that includes the prompt
  • Adds answer length and repeated-sentence rate to the evaluation, since exact match never shows this failure

2. The support lead wants to fine-tune the assistant “so it knows the new refund policy and answers in our house style”. What do you build?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Splits the request: the policy is a fact that changes, so it goes in the M11 index and is cited; the house style is behaviour, which fine-tuning can carry
  • Starts with a prompt that states the style and shows two examples, and fine-tunes only if that fails on a measured set or costs too many tokens per call
  • Builds training examples whose answers follow the style but take their facts from retrieved context, so the model learns to use context rather than memorise the policy
  • Plans for the next policy change: re-index, no retraining, and a golden question that checks the new window is cited

3. Your fine-tuned model’s training loss fell for two epochs. How do you decide whether it replaces the prompted model in production?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Evaluates on a held-out split the model never trained on (the notebooks hold out 10%) plus the M11 golden questions, never on training loss
  • Scores what was trained: the share of answers that lead with Yes or No, faithfulness to the retrieved policy, refusal rate on off-topic questions such as What do you think of Mars?
  • Runs a general benchmark (lesson 4.6 uses ARC Easy) before and after, to catch lost general ability
  • Compares against the prompted baseline on the same questions, with cost and latency per call, and reviews the regressions question by question

4. Ten percent of Larkspur’s transcripts are longer than the 512-token limit you can afford in training. What do you do with them?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Measures the length distribution first; lesson 4.4’s lamini_docs rows peak at 479 tokens, well under the 2,048 the notebook allows, so its truncation never fires
  • Knows the trade-off: right truncation cuts the end of the answer and its end token, left truncation cuts the question and the template header
  • Prefers fixing the data (drop long greetings and signatures, split multi-turn threads into single exchanges) over silently cutting
  • Logs how many rows were truncated on every run, so a change in the data shows up as a number

5. The team has one 24 GB GPU and wants to fine-tune a 7-billion-parameter model. Is that feasible, and with which settings?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Does the arithmetic: Llama 2 7B has 6.74 billion parameters, 13.5 GB of weights in 16-bit before any gradients or optimizer state, so full fine-tuning does not fit
  • Loads the base weights in 4 bits (about 3.4 GB) and trains LoRA adapters: at rank 16 on every linear layer that is 40.0 million trainable parameters, 0.59% of the model
  • Uses a LoRA-sized learning rate; AutoTrain’s default is 3e-5, and the notebook’s 0.4 is more than 13,000 times that
  • Keeps batch size small with gradient accumulation and gradient checkpointing, and merges or serves the adapter separately depending on how many variants are deployed

Key Takeaways

  • Try prompting first, put changing facts in retrieval, and fine-tune for behaviour you can describe and measure.
  • Before training, decode a few prepared rows: check the template, the end token, what truncation removed and what padding added.
  • Report training runs in examples and epochs, and read the learning-rate schedule for the steps you actually run.
  • Evaluate the fine-tuned model on held-out questions, on the behaviour you trained for, against the baseline it is meant to replace.
