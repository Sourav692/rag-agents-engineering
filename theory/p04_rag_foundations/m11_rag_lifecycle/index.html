M11 The RAG lifecycle

A language model answers from what it memorised during training. RAG adds a second source: passages retrieved from your own documents at the moment the question arrives. The M11 notebooks build on four ideas: where knowledge lives, the stages of the pipeline, how many chunks to retrieve, and why you measure a baseline before you optimise anything.

Open the lab

The running example throughout these pages and the lab is a small product handbook for a fictional cloud host, Larkspur Cloud. M12 and M13 reuse the same handbook.

TL;DR

  • RAG moves knowledge out of model weights (parametric) into an index you control (non-parametric), so you can update it, restrict it and cite it.
  • The pipeline has an offline indexing phase (load → split → embed → store) and an online query phase (retrieve → augment → generate). A mistake made at indexing time often surfaces only in the generated answer.
  • k controls how much context the model reads. It trades recall against precision, tokens and latency, and it cannot recover a chunk the retriever scores as irrelevant.
  • Build the plain pipeline first, record its numbers on a fixed question set, then change one stage at a time and compare.

Mental model

The index is the only thing the two phases share. A bad chunk written at indexing time is retrieved, quoted and cited at query time, and nothing in the query phase can repair it.

Parametric vs non-parametric knowledge

Lesson 11.1: The knowledge problem: hallucination, freshness, context-window growth, RAG vs fine-tuning vs long context

Parametric knowledge is what the model stores in its weights. It is fast and needs no infrastructure, but it has four problems for an application:

  • Stale. It stops at the training cut-off. Larkspur’s refund policy changed in March 2026; no model trained before then knows.
  • Missing. Private data (your tickets, contracts, runbooks) was never in the training set.
  • Unverifiable. The model cannot point to where a fact came from, so neither you nor the user can check it.
  • Confident when wrong. Asked about something it never saw, the model still produces fluent text. That is a hallucination.

Non-parametric knowledge lives outside the model, in an index you build and query. RAG retrieves the relevant passages and puts them in the prompt, so the model answers from evidence it can quote.

The three ways to give a model new knowledge solve different problems:

RAG Fine-tuning Long context
Where the knowledge lives External index Model weights The prompt, every call
Updating a fact Re-index one document Retrain Edit the prompt
Citations Yes, per chunk No Possible, but nothing narrows the evidence
Cost per question Retrieval + a short prompt Cheap once trained Pays for the whole corpus in tokens
Best at Facts that change, private data, auditability Style, format, domain vocabulary, behaviour Small corpora that fit, one-off analysis
Fails when Retrieval misses the evidence Facts change after training The corpus outgrows the window, or the answer sits mid-context

Fine-tuning and RAG combine well: fine-tune for tone and format, retrieve for facts. M04 covers when fine-tuning is worth it.

Top-k

Lesson 11.2: Indexing from scratch with TF-IDF and nearest neighbours. Notebooks: 02a_indexing_from_scratch.ipynb

The retriever scores every chunk against the question and returns the k best. Those k chunks are all the model sees. Two metrics describe the result against a golden set of questions whose evidence chunks you have labelled:

Recall@k    = evidence chunks in the top k / all evidence chunks for the question
Precision@k = evidence chunks in the top k / k

The interactive lab indexes twelve Larkspur handbook chunks with real TF-IDF (the same weighting scikit-learn uses) and three golden questions, each with two evidence chunks. Its numbers:

Question k = 1 k = 2 k = 5 k = 12
Can I export my data after I cancel my plan? R 0.50 · P 1.00 R 1.00 · P 1.00 R 1.00 · P 0.40 R 1.00 · P 0.17
How long does a refund take? R 0.50 · P 1.00 R 0.50 · P 0.50 R 0.50 · P 0.20 R 0.50 · P 0.08
What happens if my server goes down? R 0.00 · P 0.00 R 0.00 · P 0.00 R 0.00 · P 0.00 R 0.00 · P 0.00
Context sent (≈ tokens) ~30 ~60 ~150 ~348

Each question fails in a different way:

  • The export question is a ranking problem, and k = 2 solves it. Past that, each extra chunk is noise: precision falls from 1.00 to 0.17 while the prompt grows almost sixfold.
  • The refund question is a representation problem. The refund-timing chunk says “Refunds”; the question says “refund”. TF-IDF matches exact tokens, so that chunk scores 0 and no k retrieves it. Meanwhile reboot scores 0.194 because both contain “does”.
  • The outage question is vocabulary mismatch. The evidence talks about “outage” and “availability”; the question says “server goes down”. TF-IDF ranks reboot and sizing first because they contain “server”. Embeddings (lesson 11.3, M14) and query rewriting (M18) fix this. A bigger k only adds noise.

Raising k has costs beyond tokens. Every extra chunk adds latency and gives the model text it can misread. Long prompts also have a position effect: models use information at the start and end of the context more reliably than information in the middle (Liu et al., Lost in the Middle, 2023). Retrieve generously when a reranker (M19) will trim the list afterwards; retrieve tightly when chunks go straight to the model.

Indexing vs retrieval vs generation

Lesson 11.5: The RAG lifecycle and a measured baseline. Notebooks: 05a_rag_lifecycle_and_baseline.ipynb

The lifecycle has seven steps in two phases. Each step has one job and one typical failure.

Phase Step Job Typical failure Fixed in
Indexing Load Turn files into Documents with metadata Garbled PDF text, missing metadata M12
Indexing Split Cut documents into retrievable chunks A fact split across two chunks M13
Indexing Embed Turn each chunk into a vector Model cannot tell paraphrases apart, or blurs exact codes M14
Indexing Store Index vectors for nearest-neighbour search Chunk count ≠ vector count; no metadata filters M15
Query Retrieve Return the top-k chunks with scores Evidence ranked below the cut-off, or not at all M17 to M19
Query Augment Format the chunks into the prompt Sources dropped, so no citations M20
Query Generate Answer from the supplied context Ignores the evidence, or answers beyond it M20, M28 and M29

Baseline-first optimisation

Every later module in this course changes one stage of the pipeline: chunking, embeddings, hybrid search, reranking, query rewriting. Without a baseline, you cannot tell whether a change helped, hurt, or only moved the failure to another question.

The method:

  1. Fix a golden set. 20 to 50 real questions, each with its evidence chunks and a reference answer. Freeze it, and add questions when production shows a new failure.
  2. Build the plain pipeline. Default loader, recursive splitter, one embedding model, top-k similarity, a simple prompt.
  3. Record the baseline row. Retrieval metrics (recall@k, hit rate, MRR), answer metrics (correctness, faithfulness), latency and cost per question.
  4. Change one stage, re-run, compare. Keep the change only if the row improves on the questions it targets without breaking the others.

At k = 2 the top-k lab’s baseline row is mean recall@2 = 0.50 and mean precision@2 = 0.50. That number already points at the next modules: the export question is solved, the refund question needs better term matching (M14, M17), and the outage question needs paraphrase-aware retrieval (M14, M18).

The lifecycle notebook ends with the map from symptom to module:

What you observe Likely cause Where it is fixed
Question worded unlike the documents; evidence never retrieved Vocabulary mismatch M14 embeddings, M18 query transformation
Exact codes or names missed (ORD-88213) Dense retrieval blurs rare tokens M17 hybrid search
Half an answer; one fact found, another missed Question needs several chunks M18 query decomposition

Retrieved chunk holds half the fact Chunk boundary M13 chunking

Interview Spotlight: 5 Questions You Might Get Asked

Production questions in the style of OpenAI, Anthropic and Google interviews for applied AI roles.

1. Larkspur’s support assistant answers billing questions from the handbook. The policy team changes the refund window on Monday. A teammate proposes fine-tuning the model on the new handbook. What do you recommend, and what has to happen on Monday?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Facts that change belong in the index, not the weights; fine-tuning would need repeating at every policy change and gives no citations
  • Monday’s work is re-indexing the changed document: load, split, embed, upsert, and remove the stale chunks
  • Uses date or version metadata so retrieval can prefer the current policy and the old one can be filtered out
  • Adds the new policy question to the golden set and checks that the assistant now cites the new chunk

2. A customer asks “Can I get a refund after 45 days?” and the assistant says “No, refunds are only available within 30 days.” The handbook says refunds after day 30 are prorated. How do you find out what went wrong?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Prints the retrieved chunks with scores before touching the prompt
  • If the proration chunk was not retrieved: retrieval failure. Check its rank, whether it was split from its context, and whether “45 days” matches nothing lexically
  • If it was retrieved but ignored: generation failure. Check its position in the prompt, conflicting chunks, and the instruction to answer only from context
  • Turns the case into a golden-set question so the fix is measured and stays fixed

3. Your team runs at k = 20 “to be safe”. Latency is 3.1 s, and the product requirement is 1.5 s. How do you choose a new k without hurting quality?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Measures recall@k on the golden set for k = 1…20 and finds where the curve flattens
  • Separates the decision: retrieve wide (20 to 50), rerank, then send a small k to the model, if a reranker fits in the latency budget
  • Reports answer quality, not only recall, because fewer distractors often improves faithfulness
  • Watches per-question regressions, not just the mean; one lost question type can matter more than the average

4. The baseline went live last month. How do you know whether retrieval quality is degrading in production before users complain?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Logs every query with retrieved chunk ids, scores and the final answer
  • Tracks retrieval signals: top-1 score distribution, the share of queries below a score floor, out-of-scope fallback rate
  • Samples traffic for offline grading with an LLM judge (faithfulness, relevance) and re-runs the golden set on every index or model change
  • Alerts on drift after content updates, such as a new document type that the loader parses badly

5. Someone suggests skipping RAG because the new model has a 1M-token context and the whole handbook fits. When is that right, and when does it break?

What a strong answer covers:

  • Right for a small, stable corpus with low query volume, or for one-off analysis
  • Cost and latency scale with corpus size on every call, while RAG pays for a few chunks
  • Accuracy can fall for facts mid-context, and the model has no ranked evidence to cite
  • Access control is coarse: every user’s prompt holds every document, unless you filter per user, which is retrieval again

Key Takeaways

  • RAG answers from an index you control. Update it by re-indexing, and cite chunks instead of trusting memory.
  • Indexing happens once and is where most failures start. Always ask whether the correct passage was retrieved before you change the prompt.
  • k trades recall against precision, tokens and latency. If a chunk scores zero, no k will retrieve it; fix the representation instead.
  • Record a baseline on a frozen golden set, change one stage at a time, and keep only the changes that beat it.
